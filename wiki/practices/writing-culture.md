# Writing culture

## Overview

Some companies replace slides and talk with written narrative documents. Amazon is the best-known case: no slide presentations, six-page narrative memos read at the start of meetings. Its own account stresses that good memos are slow and costly to write. For decisions that matter, the leaner output can be one well-made document instead of a deck plus a long meeting: fewer artifacts, not fewer words.

## Amazon's six-page memos

Jeff Bezos's annual shareholder letter (practitioner; a CEO describing his own company) states: "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon." Meetings instead use "narratively structured six-page memos", read silently in a "study hall" at the start.

He does not claim memos are easy. "Not surprisingly, the quality of these memos varies widely." The best ones "are written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work", and this "simply can't be done in a day or two": "a great memo probably should take a week or more." The revision cycle includes time away from the draft: memos are "set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind." He locates weak memos in a wrong expectation of scope, not in a lack of standards: writers "mistakenly believe a high-standards, six-page memo can be written in one or two days or even a few hours,". His fix is not a template but realistic expectations: "you can improve results through the simple act of teaching scope." He states the general rule bluntly: "Unrealistic beliefs on scope – often hidden and undiscussed – kill high standards." He also says a great memo resists a written specification ("It would be extremely hard to write down the detailed requirements that make up a great memo"), yet readers "know it when they see it." Writing skill does not have to sit with the decision owner: "Someone on the team needs to have the skill, but it doesn't have to be you", and "by tradition at Amazon, authors' names never appear on the memos – the memo is from the whole team."

## Stripe: write it down, share it widely

Brie Wolfson (practitioner; a former Stripe employee writing first-person) describes a default of writing and broadcasting: "We were encouraged to write everything down and then share it to a Google Group", "whether it was a meaningful strategy document, personal musings on a topic, or a mundane email exchange." Word choice was policed (staff "were told not to use phrases like "thank you for your patience""), and "Sending meeting notes was a privilege, not a chore". Review was constant: "My work was meticulously but warmly critiqued by my peers and leaders alike".

She also records the cost: "the culture was demanding," and "I cried more than a few times after feeling like I let a user or a colleague down." A writing-heavy culture can add volume and pressure as well as clarity.

## Writing over talk

37signals' communication guide (practitioner) makes the case in slogans: "Writing solidifies, chat dissolves." "Speaking only helps who's in the room, writing helps everyone."

## Writing in distributed companies

Matt Mullenweg (practitioner; Automattic CEO) ties writing to distributed work: once a company is remote-first, "you realize just how crucial written communication is for your success." Doist's CEO Amir Salihefendic (practitioner) goes further: "Everyone needs to be a great writer in an async workplace", so companies should "Promote writing and communication as core skills to master." See [Async-first communication](async-first.md).

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Producing less counts artifacts and meetings, not only words. Replacing a deck and a discussion meeting with one narrative doc can be the lazy-correct output even if the doc is longer than any slide.
- For high-stakes decisions, cardigan should not compress a memo into bullets; it should say plainly that a good memo takes real drafting and review time (Bezos's point about scope).
- Write once, put it where people will find it: Stripe's share-to-a-group habit favours one findable post over many private copies. The same habit, applied to everything, also produces volume, so cardigan writes down decisions and answers, not every exchange.
- Narrative over slides fits cardigan's deck handling: when asked for a deck to support a decision, offering a short written narrative is a legitimate alternative.
