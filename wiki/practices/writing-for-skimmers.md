# Writing for skimmers

> Sources: Samantha Zuniga-Levy / Harvard Gazette (interviewing Todd Rogers), 2023-10-16
> Raw: [Tips on how to connect with people who don't have time to read](../../raw/research/review-practice/2023-10-16-harvard-gazette-rogers-writing-for-busy-readers.md)
> Updated: 2026-10-31

## Overview

Readers skim, and care less about a message than its writer does. Todd Rogers and Jessica Lasky-Fink, who ran field experiments on how schools and campaigns reach busy people, draw three rules: fewer words, ideas and requests; structure for jumping around; and emphasis used sparingly, because bold text pulls readers away from everything else. Each extra request makes the main one less likely to get done. The evidence here is journalism: an interview in which one of the researchers summarises the work; the experiments themselves were not reviewed.

## Readers skim

Rogers (Harvard Kennedy School), interviewed by the Harvard Gazette, says he and Lasky-Fink "have been collaborating on field experiments for around a decade, mostly about how school districts communicate with busy families and campaigns communicate with busy voters." The starting point: "our readers are not reading what we write carefully. And the vast majority of the time, they don't care as much as we do about what we write."

He puts the burden on the writer: the usual goal of being "complete, comprehensive, clear" "puts the burden on readers to make sure they pull out what we want them to pull out", whereas "The burden, if we want to achieve our goals as writers, is on us".

## Fewer words, ideas and requests

"First: Less is more. That is, fewer words, fewer ideas, fewer requests." Cutting words is "costless"; cutting "somewhat-useful-but-not-necessary ideas is harder", and "there is no right amount to cut." On asks: "the more actions a message asks of readers, the less likely readers are to do any one of them", so "adding an additional request will decrease the likelihood that the high priority one gets done."

## Structure and sparing emphasis

"Second: Add structure." Use headings and "make sure the first paragraph describes how to navigate the document. Most people aren't reading linearly; they're jumping around."

"Third: Use enough formatting, but no more." In their surveys, readers take underline, bold and highlight as the writer saying "this is the most important content." Emphasis in the middle of a message "dramatically increases the likelihood that people read that portion, but it decreases the likelihood that they read the rest of the message."

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- One main ask per message. When the user's draft has several requests, cardigan ranks them and moves secondary ones to a later message or a list the reader can ignore, rather than stacking them.
- Bold is a scarce resource: at most one emphasised item per message (usually the ask or deadline), never decorative.
- Long documents get headings and a first paragraph that says how to read them; short messages need neither.
- Cutting ideas, not just words, is a legitimate edit, as long as facts, owners, deadlines and compliance wording stay.
