## Overview

Practitioner rules for keeping outbound messages small: one screen, one topic, subject-line-only messages for single facts, and no reply at all when the reply would only say "thanks". The rules here rest on practitioner experience, not measurement (field experiments on writing for busy readers, reported second-hand, are covered in [Writing for skimmers](writing-for-skimmers.md)); the studies here show the main risk, that short, impersonal messages are read as more negative than intended, and that senders do not notice.

## The smallest forms

Merlin Mann (practitioner, 43 Folders) lists a ladder of small forms:

- **Subject line only.** For "just a single fact or asking one question", use the subject line and mark it "(EOM)—for end of message".
- **One screen.** His top "visual trick" is to "fit it onto one screen with no scrolling", and he admits this rests on "anecdotal evidence and hunches".
- **One topic.** "do not mix topics, projects, or domains of life in a given email", unless the team prefers otherwise.
- **No message.** "a one-word "Thanks" email can be crufty and unnecessary."
- **Pithy by default.** "Keep it pithy."

He ties brevity to care rather than haste: "Careless emails do not invite careful responses."

## Cut it in half

Sonja Manning (practitioner; a startup chief of staff writing first-person in First Round Review) states the same instinct as a standing self-check: "I have a sticky note on my desk that reads "Sonja, how can you cut that message by 50%?"" Her reasoning is that more is not more: "often sharing more information is actually less powerful. It's always a balance to determine the right level of context or detail to share."

Cutting stock filler is a separate move from cutting length. At Stripe, according to former employee Brie Wolfson (practitioner), staff "were told not to use phrases like "thank you for your patience"".

## Brief but explicit

GitLab's handbook (official-docs) asks for both brevity and context. "Edit for brevity and clarity, especially in one-to-many communications." "Keep it simple, brief and summarize what is important." But also: "Use low-context communications by being explicit in your communications", and "Provide as much context as possible to avoid confusion." The two fit together if brevity means cutting padding, not cutting the context the reader lacks.

## Fixed caps

The anonymous one-page site five.sentenc.es (practitioner; no byline) proposes a hard cap. Its problem statement: "E-mail takes too long to respond to, resulting in continuous inbox overflow for those who receive a lot of it." The policy: "all email responses regardless of recipient or subject will be five sentences or less. It's that simple." Variants exist for two, three and four sentences.

The site itself anticipates a tone problem. It suggests pasting a signature that explains the cap: "Q: Why is this email five sentences or less?" with a link to the policy. The need for that disclaimer is indirect evidence that unexplained brevity reads badly.

## Brevity and tone

Ted Zorn (study; a researcher's plain-language summary of his peer-reviewed work) reports that "we asked 276 adults in Australia and New Zealand who used email regularly at work" to "provide an example of an email they had received that either conveyed or prompted negative emotion." Observers then rated the same emails. The finding: "people who had received the emails directly rated the messages far more negatively than did the observers."

The gap was largest in conditions that overlap with terse writing:

- "Hyper-negative interpretations were most likely to come into play with ambiguous messages"
- and "when the messages were short and impersonal";
- it grew further with a negative organisational climate and "when the email sender was in a higher position of power."

An earlier study points to the sender's side of the same gap. NYU Stern's summary of Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng's JPSP paper *Egocentrism Over E-Mail* (study; a school's research summary, no figures given) reports: "Senders were typically overconfident that their intent was fully grasped by recipients." The explanation is egocentrism: "the sender can only operate from his or her own world at that moment." The consequence: "Overestimating the obviousness of one's intentions can lead to insufficient allowances for ambiguities in communication". A writer who trims a message cannot judge from their own reading whether the tone survived.

Power also shapes how politely people write in the first place. A computational study of online communities found that "users at the top of the reputation scale are less polite than those at the bottom" (Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil and colleagues; see [Politeness and softening](../pushback/politeness-and-softening.md)). Put next to Zorn's result, the higher-power writer tends to write less politely, and that is the writer whose messages are most often read as negative.

37signals' guide (practitioner) makes the general point as a principle: "Companies don't have communication problems, they have miscommunication problems", and "Poor communication creates more work."

Zorn's recommendation is awareness, not length rules: training should "heighten awareness of the many opportunities for misinterpretation in email", and "Acknowledging the role of power dynamics and the general climate in an organisation will also help."

> **Status: Disputed**
> The five-sentence policy applies its cap "regardless of recipient or subject" (five.sentenc.es, practitioner, undated). Zorn's study (2021) finds that recipients read emails as more negative when they are "short and impersonal" and "when the email sender was in a higher position of power", so recipient and context matter to how a short reply lands. The practitioner rule is about the sender's time; the study is about the recipient's reading. The policy's own signature disclaimer concedes part of Zorn's point.

## Length and perceived standing

Trade-press coverage of research by Jen Overbeck of Melbourne Business School (journalism; INTHEBLACK, CPA Australia's magazine) adds a status angle. The research looked at "linguistic cues in relation to specific concepts of status such as power, dominance and prestige", "to find out whether the power someone tries to project in their writing matches how others actually perceive it." Reported findings:

- "using analytical language is positively associated with perceived power and prestige."
- "longer emails are positively associated with prestige."
- "expressing negative emotions correlates with being perceived as less powerful."

Overbeck's advice in the article is about emotion, not length: "reining in those negative emotions is a really important thing to do." The coverage gives no sample size or method beyond the linguistic-cue description, and the associations are correlational.

> **Status: Disputed**
> The brevity rules above ("Keep it pithy", Mann; "how can you cut that message by 50%?", Manning; five sentences or fewer, five.sentenc.es; all practitioner) treat shorter as better by default. Overbeck's research, as reported in INTHEBLACK (journalism, 2026), finds that "longer emails are positively associated with prestige" and that analytical language is associated with perceived power. The two can be reconciled if what lengthens a respected email is reasoning rather than padding, but the coverage does not say which, so the conflict stands.

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Mann's list maps directly onto cardigan's ladder: nothing (skip the thanks-only reply) → subject line only → one screen → longer only when needed. One topic per message means a mixed request becomes separate messages or threads.
- A sentence cap or the one-screen rule is a default, not a hard or evidence-backed limit (the one-screen rule is a hunch by its author's account). Facts, owners, deadlines, compliance wording and the ask are never cut to fit a count.
- Short is safe only when it is unambiguous. A terse reply from a manager to a report is the case most likely to be misread, so cardigan keeps one warm or context clause there even at its tersest setting. Because senders overrate how clearly their intent comes through (Kruger and colleagues), that clause states the tone (an acknowledgment, a reason) rather than trusting that goodwill is implied.
- Stock filler ("thank you for your patience" and similar) can go without losing warmth; a specific warm clause is worth more than a generic one. Trims remove padding but keep the reasoning: analytical content is linked to perceived standing (Overbeck), and irritation is never worth the words it saves.
- Brevity cuts padding, not context the reader lacks (GitLab's brief-but-explicit rule). *Can this be cut by half?* (after Manning's sticky note) is a useful revision pass, as long as the never-cut items survive it.
