# Proportional review

> Sources: Benjamin Mortimer / Government Digital Service, 2017-02-06; Jeff Bezos / Amazon, 2018-04-18; Aakash Gupta (expanding Shreyas Doshi), 2022-02-01; Hurley Write, 2024-02-27
> Raw: [Talk more, queue less: faster updates for GOV.UK](../../raw/research/review-practice/2017-02-06-govuk-talk-more-queue-less-2i-review.md); [2017 Letter to Shareholders](../../raw/research/review-practice/2018-04-18-bezos-2017-shareholder-letter-memo-standards.md); [LNO Framework for Product Managers](../../raw/research/review-practice/2022-02-01-aakash-gupta-lno-framework.md); [Optimizing Your Team's Editing and Review Process](../../raw/research/review-practice/2024-02-27-hurley-write-optimizing-editing-review.md)
> Updated: 2026-10-31

## Overview

How much review a piece of writing gets should depend on what it is for. A mandatory review step for everything turns into a queue; moving review earlier and letting simple work skip steps keeps quality without the wait. At the top of the scale, decision memos for a leadership meeting justify days of rewriting and peer review (Amazon); at the bottom, the LNO framework says to aim for a deliberately average job on routine messages. The evidence is first-hand practitioner accounts and practitioner frameworks, not measurement.

## When mandatory review becomes a queue

Benjamin Mortimer (practitioner; GDS content designer) explains why GOV.UK requires a second reader: "when you spend time writing a piece of content it becomes harder to imagine yourself as a user reading it for the first time." But the rule outgrew its capacity: "we ended up with far more people producing content than we had people trained to 2i it." Content bounced back and forth, and "Changes could take a long time to go live."

## Review earlier, and less for small work

The fix was to "review as we go": before writing, "you ask another content designer to have a conversation with you about it", agreeing the approach first. The final review still happens, but "more content flies straight through 2i and is published first time round. When things do come back, amends are much smaller." The stated main benefit is "failing earlier", spotting "that an approach won't work at the earliest possible point".

The same post records the cost for small items: "Explaining all the background to someone else can take much longer than just writing the content would, without a very different outcome." So "If you're working on something simple, you can skip one of the chats". It also notes that "It can also be daunting to receive a long page of written feedback".

## The high end: decision memos

Jeff Bezos (practitioner; a CEO describing his own company) sets the top of the scale. Amazon's meetings run on "narratively structured six-page memos", and "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind. They simply can't be done in a day or two." Weak memos, he argues, come from "a wrong expectation on scope": writers believe the memo "can be written in one or two days or even a few hours, when really it might take a week or more!" His rule is that realistic scope must be taught: "you must explicitly coach realistic scope."

This is a claim about one kind of document, read silently by a whole meeting before a decision. See [Writing culture](../practices/writing-culture.md).

## Leverage, neutral, overhead

Aakash Gupta (practitioner; product leader) expands Shreyas Doshi's LNO framework, quoting Doshi's original advice: "Seek leverage, actively try to do a bad job for certain tasks." (Doshi's thread was not fetched; his words come through Gupta.) Tasks fall into three bands:

- **Leverage** tasks "10x your impact". "Feel free to keep iterating on the work, driving towards perfection. You want the A+."
- **Neutral** tasks "offer regular impact in exchange for time". "This work has to be done, but PMs do not need to edit grammar on slack messages." "Aim for C+/B- when encountering these types of tasks." The common error: "to consider neutral work leveraged, because of their perfectionist natures."
- **Overhead** tasks "have very little impact for the time they take". "Overhead work is the type of work you need to actively try to do a bad job." "Just get the work done."

Gupta notes the social cost: "If everyone is pouring their heart and soul into an Overhead task and you aren't, the pressure to conform will be real."

Read together with Bezos, the two are compatible: a six-page decision memo is leverage work in LNO terms, and a Slack reply is neutral work.

## Good enough, and over-editing

Hurley Write (practitioner; a business-writing training firm, so it has a commercial interest in review processes) argues that review "can easily turn wasteful, unhelpful, and unproductive", and that it needs balance "without inflating the process into a bloated mess." Its rule of thumb is a Pareto claim, not a measurement: "you can reach "good enough" with just 20% of the effort it takes to reach "perfect."" So the skill is to "discern times when perfection is worth the investment of effort (perhaps a proposal that would elevate the business into a new playing field) versus when "good enough" is enough", and otherwise: "don't over-edit; limit edits to the strictly necessary."

Its cautionary case is one client anecdote: "a manager who spent an average of 16 hours a week (nearly half his workweek!) editing work produced by his engineers", which "overloaded him and created a lot of stress and frustration among his team."

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Review intensity is a dial set by stakes, not a fixed step; LNO's three bands are a usable default classification (leverage: polish; neutral: good enough; overhead: minimum). For a routine message cardigan skips its own review pass beyond the never-cut checks; for a decision document with a wide or senior audience it reviews properly, and says honestly that a good one takes days, not minutes (Bezos's scope point).
- For a big document, the cheapest review is of the plan: cardigan confirms the ask, audience and outline in a line or two before drafting, rather than polishing a draft that answers the wrong question.
- When cardigan asks a colleague to review something, the request should match the item's size: a quick look for a small change, not a full briefing.
- Feedback cardigan writes on someone else's work should be short, ranked and limited to what is strictly necessary; a long page of comments has its own cost, and a reviewer who rewrites everything becomes the bottleneck (Hurley's 16-hours-a-week manager).
