# Recipient check

> Sources: Corrs Chambers Westgarth, 2021-04-21; Osterman Research, 2025-03-27
> Raw: [The increased danger of 'reply all': a warning for in-house counsel](../../raw/research/review-practice/2021-04-21-corrs-reply-all-danger-in-house-counsel.md); [Misdirected communications – 2024 update from the ICO](../../raw/research/review-practice/2025-03-27-osterman-ico-misdirected-email-2024.md)
> Updated: 2026-10-31

## Overview

Who receives a message is part of its content. Sending to the wrong person is the most common data security incident reported to the UK regulator, and reply-all or forwarding long chains widen the audience without anyone deciding to, with possible legal consequences: under an Australian ruling, legal privilege attaches only to the copy sent to the lawyer. The evidence is an analyst firm's summary of regulator data (vendor-data) and a law firm's practitioner briefing on one jurisdiction.

## Misdirected email is the common failure

Osterman Research (vendor-data; an analyst firm summarising UK Information Commissioner's Office figures, whose own dashboard was not fetched) reports that "The most common data security incident reported to the Information Commissioner's Office (UK) for October to December 2024 was … unsurprisingly, misdirected emails." "Of the total incident count reported to the ICO, 21% were of this type." It blames "the ease of stumbling when using type-ahead addressing in Outlook and other email clients, and the frenetic pace of much office work".

Training helps only partly: users should "check and double check when adding someone to an email message or distribution list, but that's not guaranteed to work in all instances." The cost scales with the content: "Banal stuff … not so much. Corporate IP, confidential data, and data subject to privacy regulations … much more so. Excel spreadsheets with customer information – yes, that's a problem." Hence "The higher the risk, the more layered a mitigation approach should be."

## Each recipient gets a separate communication

Corrs Chambers Westgarth (practitioner; an Australian law firm briefing in-house lawyers on a court decision) warns that "Privilege is an important legal right but it is also very easy to waive." Under the approach the case follows, "if an email is sent to 10 recipients, one of whom is a lawyer, only the copy of the email that goes to the lawyer will be privileged (assuming it seeks legal advice). The remaining copies of the email will not be privileged even though they contain exactly the same content". And "It is a common misconception that copying a lawyer on an email is sufficient for a communication to attract privilege."

Threads drift, too: "Email chains that evolve from having a legal purpose to having a non-legal (or mixed) purpose may now result in a waiver of privilege of legal advice contained in the chain."

## What the firm recommends

- Choose recipients deliberately: "Identify who actually needs access to your advice and consider emailing them individually."
- Prefer a fresh chain: "consider starting a new email chain rather than forwarding lengthy chains to multiple colleagues." This "may also help prevent lengthy email chains circulating around your organisation."
- Keep one purpose per email: ask colleagues "to restrict the purpose of their emails with you to that sole purpose."

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Recipients are on the never-cut list in reverse: cardigan never silently adds or keeps recipients. Before sending a reply-all or a forward, it states who will receive it, and flags recipients who do not need the content.
- When a thread mixes legal advice with other business, cardigan suggests splitting it into a new, single-purpose email to the people who need the advice, and does not forward the advice itself further without saying so.
- The recipient check scales with content, like the rest of review: a quick glance for routine mail, an explicit named-recipient confirmation when the message carries personal data, confidential material or a spreadsheet attachment. Autocompleted addresses are the specific thing to check.
- cardigan does not give legal advice on privilege; it flags the risk and leaves the call to the user or their counsel. The cited ruling is Australian.
