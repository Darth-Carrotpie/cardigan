# Keep promises, don't exceed them

> Sources: David Jon Phillips / Chicago Booth Review (reporting on Epley and Gneezy), 2014-11-17
> Raw: [Don't Bother Doing More Than You Promised](../../raw/research/pushback/2014-11-17-chicago-booth-dont-bother-doing-more-than-promised.md)
> Updated: 2026-10-31

## Overview

Delivering more than was agreed feels like it should earn goodwill. Lab experiments reported by Chicago Booth Review suggest it does not: breaking a promise is penalised, keeping it is valued, and going beyond it adds nothing the other person notices. The extra effort in an over-delivered report or deck mostly goes unrewarded, while under-delivering on the actual ask is costly.

## The experiments

Chicago Booth Review (journalism, reporting Epley and Gneezy's paper in *Social Psychological and Personality Science*) summarises the result: "Breaking a promise is costly, but exceeding it might not be worth the effort." In one study, "each study participant was paired with a volunteer who promised to solve 10 of the puzzles." The volunteers then broke, kept or exceeded that promise.

The findings:

- "As expected, participants valued keeping a promise more highly than breaking one."
- "However, students said exceeded promises made them no happier."
- The extra effort was not even seen: "in their view, people who exceeded their promises worked no harder than people who simply kept promises."

The article draws the workplace conclusion itself: "The research suggests that your boss will be just as happy if you simply fulfill your promise". The evidence is small-stakes lab tasks with students, reported second-hand; the article's own hedge is that exceeding "may not produce the desired consequence beyond those obtained by simply keeping promises".

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Deliver what was asked, at the quality asked. The bigger-than-requested version (extra sections, a bonus appendix, a deck on top of the memo) mostly costs the user's time without buying goodwill.
- The asymmetry cuts the other way too: under-delivering on the actual ask is penalised. This is why the ask itself is on cardigan's never-cut list; cardigan trims extras, never the promised core.
- When cardigan proposes a lighter version than the one requested, that changes the promise, so it asks or states the change rather than silently delivering less.
