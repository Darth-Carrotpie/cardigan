# Saying no

> Sources: Scott Eblin / Government Executive, 2010-12-14; Will Larson / Irrational Exuberance, 2018-09-23; Linda Babcock, Maria P. Recalde, Lise Vesterlund, Laurie Weingart, undated (American Economic Review, 2017); Evidence Based Living / Cornell University (quoting Vanessa Bohns), undated
> Raw: [How to Push Back on Your Boss](../../raw/research/pushback/2010-12-14-eblin-how-to-push-back-on-your-boss.md); [Saying no.](../../raw/research/pushback/2018-09-23-larson-saying-no.md); [Gender Differences in Accepting and Receiving Requests for Tasks with Low Promotability](../../raw/research/pushback/babcock-gender-low-promotability-task-requests.md); [You Have More Power Over Others Than You Realize](../../raw/research/pushback/bohns-you-have-more-power-than-you-realize.md)

## Overview

Practitioner advice on refusing or reshaping a request agrees on a few moves: find the goal behind the request, offer an alternative that meets that goal, keep it short, and save pushback for things that matter. Saying no well usually means explaining constraints and trade-offs against shared priorities, not using the word itself. When explanations keep failing, the problem is the relationship, not the wording. Refusing is harder than requesters think, so people over-agree. Not everyone can refuse at the same cost: research on low-promotability tasks finds women are asked more often and expected to say yes.

## Start from the goal

Scott Eblin (practitioner; executive coach writing in Government Executive) begins with motive, not the request: "Do your best to understand the goal or motivation behind the boss's request." That is what makes alternatives possible: "If you understand the true motivation, you're in a better position to offer alternative courses of action." The pitch is outcome-based: "Explain how one or more of those alternatives will accomplish the goal as well as if not better than the request."

He explains why bosses ask for too much: "they're moving at 350 miles per hour and haven't stopped to consider the impact of what they're asking for." The pushback itself must be brief: "If you want to be heard, you're going to have to keep it short enough to keep their attention."

## Explain constraints, not refusals

Will Larson (practitioner; engineering executive writing on his blog) opens with the observation that "Folks who communicate “no” effectively are not the firmest speakers nor do they make frequent use of the word itself." What they do instead: "This “no” is explaining your team’s constraints to folks outside the team."

He names two recurring challenges. "The first is velocity: why is this taking so long when it should take a couple of hours?" For that, "Instead, you have to translate the problem into something resembling data." "The other is prioritization: why can’t you work on this other, more important, project?" For that, the team needs agreed rules: "From there, you have to pick the guiding principles you’ll use for selecting tasks." A new request is then tested against them together: "Understand their requests, and sit down with them to test their ask against your guiding principles." A request that wins displaces something, on a schedule: "If it’s more important than your current work, shift priorities at your next planning session."

If the same person keeps rejecting the explanation, Larson reads that as a different kind of problem: "it’s fairly likely that you have a relationship problem to address."

## Pick your battles

Eblin treats pushback as a limited budget. "Use your holds judiciously and save them for big things." "Fold on the little things." Pushing back on everything has a cost: "you'll eventually get shut out of the decision making process."

## Why no is hard

A Cornell outreach blog summarising Vanessa Bohns's research (study, reported second-hand) explains why people over-agree. Requesters misjudge the pressure they create: "we underestimate how difficult it is for people to say no to us." Yet "When we are on the receiving end of a request, we know how incredibly awkward and uncomfortable it is to say no," because "We worry about hurting someone's feelings". The result: "people often feel pressured to agree to do things they don't necessarily feel comfortable with".

In one study, participants asked strangers to lend them a phone and "only needed to ask six people"; "about half of the strangers who were approached agreed to let study participants use their phones." The post notes one limit by channel: "Over e-mail, we need to work harder to establish trust and get people's attention."

## Who is expected to say yes

Babcock, Recalde, Vesterlund and Weingart (study; peer-reviewed economics paper in the American Economic Review) look at "a task that everyone prefers be completed by someone else (writing a report, serving on a committee, etc.)". They "find evidence that women, more than men, volunteer, are asked to volunteer, and accept requests to volunteer for such tasks." The driver is expectation: "Beliefs that women, more than men, say yes to tasks with low promotability appear as an important driver of these differences." The career cost follows: "If women hold tasks that are less promotable than those held by men, then women will progress more slowly in organizations."

The paper's background establishes that such work is seen as low value. In "a survey of 48 Carnegie Mellon faculty", "89.6 percent ranked research paper and conferences as more important than curriculum committee and faculty senate." It also cites Porter (2007): "female faculty spend 15 percent more hours on committee work than do men."

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Any lighter alternative cardigan proposes is pitched against the requester's goal (*this gets you X*), not against the request (*this is too much*).
- Pushback is rationed. cardigan does not question small requests; it just does them small. The question is saved for large deliverables where the saving is real, and it obeys the short-message rules: one or two sentences, with the alternative attached.
- A bare refusal becomes a visible trade-off tied to shared priorities: *this would push back X; do you want that?* (after Larson). The decision stays with the requester.
- Refusing is hard (Bohns) and its cost is uneven (Babcock and colleagues). cardigan never assumes the user can simply decline; it offers deliverable-first options (a lighter version, a trade-off question) that do not depend on the user's standing. Requests cardigan drafts for the user carry more pressure than they seem to, so they leave an easy way out (*no worries if not*).
- Repeated friction with one requester is a signal to raise the relationship or priorities directly (a conversation, a planning session), not to push back harder on each item.
