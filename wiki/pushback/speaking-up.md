# Speaking up (employee voice)

> Sources: Susan Gilbert / HBS Working Knowledge (interview with Amy Edmondson and James Detert), 2006-03-20; Ethan R. Burris, undated (Academy of Management Journal, 2012); Michael Zipursky / Consulting Success (interview with Charles H. Green), undated

## Overview

Questioning a request from someone above you is "voice" in organisational research. People stay silent far more often than managers assume, mainly out of fear of offending those above them, and they speak up only when it feels both safe and worthwhile. The fear has a basis: managers rate employees who challenge current practice as worse performers than those whose voice is supportive. With clients, advisors argue the opposite risk matters more: an advisor who stops telling the truth loses the role, so the question is how to say it, not whether. Any pushback cardigan suggests works against this strong default toward silence.

## Silence is the default

In an interview about their fieldwork on employee silence (study; researchers summarising their own work in an e-mail interview with HBS Working Knowledge), Amy Edmondson and James Detert say: "Thus, fear of offending those above us is both natural and widespread." They were struck by how common it was: "Most surprising to us has been the degree to which fear appears to be a feature of modern work life." Given that, "it isn't surprising that people clam up when the signals seem unfavorable."

Their condition for speaking up has two parts: "In short, voice must be seen as both safe and worthwhile." A question that feels risky, or that seems unlikely to change anything, does not get asked.

## Managers set the signals

The authors put the burden on the person receiving voice. "Leader behavior is one such contextual cue." Silence misleads the boss: "No news is not good news, from the point of view of senior management, or even bosses all the way down." So "This makes facilitating voice every manager's job", and "Ultimately, every manager needs to work at being open and accessible."

## Challenging voice is penalised

Ethan Burris (study; peer-reviewed, Academy of Management Journal) tested "whether managerial responses to employees speaking up depend on the type of voice exhibited". "In one field study and two experimental studies", he found "that managers view employees who engage in more challenging forms of voice as worse performers" and that they "endorse their ideas less than those who engage in supportive forms of voice." The mechanism runs through how the speaker is seen: "Further, perceptions of loyalty and threat mediated these relationships, but in different ways."

This is the strongest evidence in this topic for the social cost of pushback, and it fits Edmondson and Detert's account of silence: the fear they describe is not irrational. Their prescription (managers should invite voice) and Burris's finding (managers often punish the challenging kind) describe the same gap from two sides.

## Candour with clients

Charles H. Green (practitioner; co-author of The Trusted Advisor, interviewed on the Consulting Success podcast) holds that client advisors must disagree when they see a problem: "You do need to tell them what you think, but how you do it makes a huge difference." Backing off is the bigger danger: "If you start backing off on truth-telling, you're in trouble." His standard for honesty is high: "you'd be willing to recommend a competitor if it were the right thing to do."

The how is private and humble. "Don't do it in front of a room of people. Do it one-on-one privately." "You can play humble, 'I can be wrong about this but it occurs to me,' or 'With all due respect.'" This matches Meyer's advice on private pre-meetings (see [Politeness and softening](politeness-and-softening.md)).

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- Users will tend to comply silently with a manager's request, even when they suspect a smaller deliverable would do. A pushback suggestion has to be cheap and low-risk to be used at all: one line, with the work already attached.
- Questioning whether a requested deliverable should exist is challenging voice. cardigan frames it as support for the requester's goal (*to get you X faster, here is a lighter version*), not as a critique of the request (after Burris).
- *Safe and worthwhile* is a usable test. cardigan suggests questioning a request only when the saving is worth it (a big deliverable, not a small one) and the phrasing carries little risk.
- With clients, a *you may not need all of this* suggestion is part of good service, not a breach of it (after Green), provided it is private, hedged and comes with the full work still on offer.
- When the user is the manager, cardigan can make the team's voice easier, for example by asking in the request itself whether a lighter version would do.
