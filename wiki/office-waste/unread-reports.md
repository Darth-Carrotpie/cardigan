# Unread reports

> Sources: Merrell Tuck-Primdahl / World Bank, 2014-05-22
> Raw: [Of report downloads and impact: Tales of a trending working paper](../../raw/research/office-waste/2014-05-22-world-bank-report-downloads-and-impact.md)
> Updated: 2026-10-31

## Overview

The best hard evidence found so far for "documents nobody reads" comes from the World Bank's own study of its policy reports, which found a large share in its sample were never downloaded. The Bank's response is that downloads are the wrong measure: many reports were never meant for wide readership, and a survey says most led to policy changes. The lesson is that readership counts and usefulness are different questions.

## The download finding

A World Bank blog post (official-docs; the institution writing about its own working paper) reports that Doemeland and Trevino's study found that "31 percent of ESW and TA reports in their sample". The rest of that sentence, that these reports were never downloaded, and the study's sample size appear in this raw note only in the collector's paraphrase.

## The impact rebuttal

The same post argues that low downloads understate value:

- Audience: "Several of the ESW and TA reports Doemeland and Trevino studied were made public under the Bank's Access to Information Policy and were never originally intended for wide dissemination."
- Aggregate reach: "Altogether, our knowledge content received more than 3.4 million downloads during the past two years alone."
- Impact: citing a survey, it says "more than three quarters of them have actually led to changes in policies, regulations, or institutions,".

> **Status: Disputed**
> The working paper by Doemeland and Trevino, as reported by the World Bank blog (2014-05-22), found that "31 percent of ESW and TA reports in their sample" (never downloaded, per the collector's paraphrase of the sentence). The blog's author, defending the Bank, argues that many such reports "were never originally intended for wide dissemination" and that "more than three quarters of them have actually led to changes in policies, regulations, or institutions,". Both claims come from the same institution; the impact figure rests on a survey whose method is not described in the raw note. The disagreement is about the metric: downloads measure broad readership, not whether the intended reader used the report.

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- cardigan's test for a report is *does the intended reader need this, and in this form?*, not *will many people read it?* A report for one decision-maker can be worth writing; a report for everyone with no named reader is the one to question.
- Before producing a long report, cardigan asks who the reader is and what they will do with it. No named reader and no decision is a reason to propose a short note, a link, or nothing.
- Publishing widely is not the same as being read. Putting a report on a shared drive does not replace sending the one reader the part they need (with a link to the rest).
- The 31 percent figure describes World Bank policy reports in one sample; cardigan's docs should not generalise it into *a third of all reports go unread*.
