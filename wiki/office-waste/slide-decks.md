# Slide decks

> Sources: Elisabeth Bumiller / The New York Times, 2010-04-26; Jeff Bezos / Amazon, 2018-04-18; Microsoft WorkLab, 2025-06-17
> Raw: [We Have Met the Enemy and He Is PowerPoint](../../raw/research/office-waste/2010-04-26-nyt-we-have-met-the-enemy-and-he-is-powerpoint.md); [2017 Letter to Shareholders](../../raw/research/office-waste/2018-04-18-amazon-2017-shareholder-letter-six-page-memos.md); [Breaking Down the Infinite Workday](../../raw/research/office-waste/2025-06-17-microsoft-wti-breaking-down-infinite-workday.md)
> Updated: 2026-10-31

## Overview

The case against the slide deck as the default format for explaining and deciding. Senior military officers quoted by the New York Times say bullet slides create an illusion of understanding and cannot carry interconnected problems; Amazon replaced slides with narrative memos read in the meeting. Neither source says the replacement is less work. The evidence is practitioner testimony and journalism, not measurement.

## The illusion of understanding

The New York Times report (journalism, reproduced by History News Network) collects US military complaints about slide briefings. The speakers are identified in the collector's note, not in the quoted excerpts: Gen. McChrystal, shown a dense Afghanistan strategy slide, said "When we understand that slide, we'll have won the war,"; Gen. Mattis said "PowerPoint makes us stupid,". The report's two core objections:

- Slides feel like comprehension without delivering it: "It's dangerous because it can create the illusion of understanding and the illusion of control,"
- Some subjects do not fit the format: "Some problems in the world are not bullet-izable."

Per the collector's note, Brig. Gen. H.R. McMaster banned PowerPoint briefings in a 2005 operation; that detail is not in the quoted text.

## Replacing the deck with prose

Jeff Bezos's 2017 shareholder letter (practitioner; a CEO on his own company) states the rule: "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon." "Instead, we write narratively structured six-page memos." Reading time is built into the meeting: "We silently read one at the beginning of each meeting in a kind of "study hall."" The prose replacement is not cheaper: a good memo "might take a week or more!" See [Writing culture](../practices/writing-culture.md).

## Deck work as last-minute activity

Microsoft's Work Trend Index special report (vendor-data; aggregated Microsoft 365 telemetry) finds that "in the final 10 minutes before a meeting, PowerPoint edits spike 122%". The report presents this as part of a pattern of reactive work, not as a measure of deck quality. See [Message overload](message-overload.md).

## For cardigan

Interpretation, not source claims:

- When asked for a deck, cardigan first asks whether the content is a decision, an explanation of causes, or a status. For causal or interconnected subjects, a short prose doc is the default alternative; for status, a few bullets in a message may be enough and no deck is needed.
- Swapping a deck for a memo is a format choice, not a promise of less effort (Bezos). cardigan says so when the decision is high-stakes.
- If a deck is genuinely required (a board pack, an external pitch), cardigan builds the smallest deck that carries the point: one claim per slide, no decorative slides.
- A meeting that will read a doc should have the reading time in the meeting (study hall) rather than an unread pre-read.
