## Excerpts

> The dirty little secret, of course, is that you don't do it by responding to each of those emails but by ruthlessly processing them.

> there's an easy but non-obvious way to win at this Catch-22: you cheat. You don't answer them all. Not even most of them.

> If you've got more than a few hundred messages in the backlog, I wouldn't even think about responding just yet (we'll agree to disagree in this instance, David Allen). Even applying GTD's "two minute rule" you might be looking at hours and hours of response time on top of your more-important culling, thinning, and deleting.

> Delete the obvious spam, chain letters, and kitty photos. Archive the mailing lists and blog comments [...] all the while identifying, flagging, and relocating all the actual important stuff to a "pending" folder

> Only when you're at zero do you return to "pending," concentrating on short responses and generation of to-dos.

> That's right. I delete them. Does this make me a bad person? Only for a second. [...] this is where I draw the line in the sand -- it's the absolute last chance for a response.

> Think in shovels
> not in teaspoons.

> Give each message as much attention as it needs and not one iota more.

Related post in the same series ("Inbox Zero: What's the action here?", 2006-03-20, http://www.43folders.com/2006/03/20/action):

> What does this message mean to me, and why do I care?
> What action, if any, does this message require of me?
> What's the most elegant way to close out this message and the nested action it contains?

> Fifty percent or more of your mail may not make it past the first question: delete. A majority of the remainder may not make it past the second (beyond perhaps a one- or two-line reply).

## Collector's note

Merlin Mann's original Inbox Zero posts on triage. The method is to process rather than answer, delete or archive most mail, keep a "pending" pile for real responses, and favour short replies. Mann openly departs from GTD's two-minute rule when the backlog is large, and admits deleting stale mail older than 60 days. The "50% delete" figure is his estimate, not measured. For cardigan it backs "no reply" and "one- or two-line reply" as the usual outcomes, plus triage questions ("what action, if any") before drafting.
