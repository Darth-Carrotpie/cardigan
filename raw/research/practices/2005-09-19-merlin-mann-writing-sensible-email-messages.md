## Excerpts

> Before you type anything into a new message, have explicit answers for two questions: Why am I writing this? What exactly do I want the result of this message to be?

> If you can't succinctly state these answers, you might want to hold off on sending your message until you can. [...] Careless emails do not invite careful responses.

> I'd propose that there are really just three basic types of business email. Providing information [...] Requesting information [...] Requesting action

> It should be clear to your recipient which type of email yours is; don't bury the lede.

> Keep it pithy, and assume that no one will ever read more than the first sentence of anything you write.

> if you're relating just a single fact or asking one question in your email, consider using just the subject line to relate your message. [...] such emails are identified by adding (EOM)—for end of message—at the end of the Subject line.

> I have only anecdotal evidence and hunches to prove this point, I'd wager that there's one visual trick most likely to improve your message's success: fit it onto one screen with no scrolling.

> If your message includes any kind of request [...] put that request near the top of the message and clearly state when you will need it.

> how many minutes have you wasted panning through a sloppy "project update" email only to completely miss the changed deadline or work request buried in the penultimate paragraph?

> Never mix, never worry - Unless your team really prefers to work that way, do not mix topics, projects, or domains of life in a given email.

> No thanks - I'm not married to this one [...] a one-word "Thanks" email can be crufty and unnecessary.

> A reminder never hurts - If you're following-up or responding to an email that's more than a few days old, provide context right at the opening.

## Collector's note

The Inbox Zero author's guide to outbound email. It covers knowing the desired result before writing, three email types (inform, request info, request action), the ask and deadline at the top, subject-only/EOM messages, one screen with no scrolling, one topic per email, and skipping "thanks"-only replies. Mann says the one-screen rule rests on "anecdotal evidence and hunches". For cardigan it reads almost like a ready-made rule set, including the "nothing" option (no thank-you reply) and the "subject line only" option, and it names the failure cardigan guards against: a deadline buried in a status email.
