# Are your work emails offensive without you even realising it? Here's how to limit negativity

> Source: https://www.smartcompany.com.au/people-human-resources/email-etiquette-work-communication/
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2021-08-26 (republished from The Conversation)
> Author: Theodore E. (Ted) Zorn, Professor of Organisational Communication, Massey University
> Type: study

## Excerpts

> we asked 276 adults in Australia and New Zealand who used email regularly at work

> provide an example of an email they had received that either conveyed or prompted negative emotion.

> people who had received the emails directly rated the messages far more negatively than did the observers.

> Hyper-negative interpretations were most likely to come into play with ambiguous messages

> when the messages were short and impersonal

> The difference was even greater when the participant's organisation had a climate

> when the email sender was in a higher position of power.

> Communication training should aim to heighten awareness of the many opportunities for misinterpretation in email

> Acknowledging the role of power dynamics and the general climate in an organisation will also help.

## Collector's note

A researcher's plain-language summary of a peer-reviewed study (linked DOI 10.1177/0893318920979828, Management Communication Quarterly). 276 Australian and New Zealand email users submitted negative emails they had received, which independent observers then rated. Recipients rated the messages much more negatively than observers did, especially when messages were ambiguous, short and impersonal, when the sender held more power, and when the organisational climate was negative. This is the main opposing evidence for cardigan's brevity: a terse reply from a manager is the case most likely to be read as hostile, so a short reply should stay unambiguous and keep one warm or contextual clause.
