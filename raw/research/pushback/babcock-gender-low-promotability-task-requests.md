# Gender Differences in Accepting and Receiving Requests for Tasks with Low Promotability

> Source: https://sites.pitt.edu/~vester/aer_promotability.pdf
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: Unknown
> Author: Linda Babcock, Maria P. Recalde, Lise Vesterlund, Laurie Weingart (American Economic Review 107(3): 714–747, 2017)
> Type: study

## Excerpts

> We examine the allocation of a task that everyone prefers be completed by someone else (writing a report, serving on a committee, etc.) and find evidence that women, more than men, volunteer, are asked to volunteer, and accept requests to volunteer for such tasks. Beliefs that women, more than men, say yes to tasks with low promotability appear as an important driver of these differences. If women hold tasks that are less promotable than those held by men, then women will progress more slowly in organizations.

> We find in a survey of 48 Carnegie Mellon faculty that there is broad agreement that promotion is more likely when more time is spent on research and less time is spent on service.

> Of those surveyed, 89.6 percent ranked research paper and conferences as more important than curriculum committee and faculty senate.

> Finally, Porter (2007) finds in the National Survey of Postsecondary Faculty (NSOPF) that female faculty spend 15 percent more hours on committee work than do men.

## Collector's note

A peer-reviewed AER paper combining lab experiments and field data. Women volunteer for, are asked to do, and accept "non-promotable" tasks (reports, committees) more often than men, largely because everyone expects them to say yes. The authors' later book, The No Club (2022), builds on this. For cardigan: who can safely push back is not evenly distributed. The expectation of a yes, and the cost of refusing, depend on the user's position, so the skill should offer light-touch, deliverable-first options instead of assuming every user can afford to decline. It also shows that low-value report and committee work is a recognised drain worth trimming.
