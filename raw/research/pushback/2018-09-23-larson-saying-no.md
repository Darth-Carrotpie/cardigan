# Saying no.

> Source: https://lethain.com/saying-no/
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2018-09-23
> Author: Will Larson (Irrational Exuberance)
> Type: practitioner

## Excerpts

> Folks who communicate “no” effectively are not the firmest speakers nor do they make frequent use of the word itself.

> This “no” is explaining your team’s constraints to folks outside the team.

> The first is velocity: why is this taking so long when it should take a couple of hours?

> Instead, you have to translate the problem into something resembling data.

> The other is prioritization: why can’t you work on this other, more important, project?

> From there, you have to pick the guiding principles you’ll use for selecting tasks.

> Understand their requests, and sit down with them to test their ask against your guiding principles.

> If it’s more important than your current work, shift priorities at your next planning session.

> [...] it’s fairly likely that you have a relationship problem to address.

## Collector's note

Will Larson, an engineering executive and author of An Elegant Puzzle and Staff Engineer, argues that saying no well rarely involves the word "no". You explain constraints with data and test the request against agreed priorities, and if explanations keep failing, treat that as a relationship problem. For cardigan: replace a bare refusal with a visible trade-off ("this would displace X; want that?") tied to shared priorities. Repeated friction with one requester is a signal to invest in the relationship rather than push back harder.
