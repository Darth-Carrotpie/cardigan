# What your work emails say about how others perceive you

> Source: https://intheblack.cpaaustralia.com.au/careers-and-workplace/what-your-work-emails-say-about-how-others-perceive-you
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2026-02-02
> Author: Megan Breen, INTHEBLACK (CPA Australia), reporting on research by Jen Overbeck (Melbourne Business School)
> Type: journalism

## Excerpts

> The research analysed linguistic cues in relation to specific concepts of status such as power, dominance and prestige.

> to find out whether the power someone tries to project in their writing matches how others actually perceive it.

> expressing negative emotions correlates with being perceived as less powerful.

> using analytical language is positively associated with perceived power and prestige.

> longer emails are positively associated with prestige.

> Consciously or unconsciously, we tailor our communication to reflect how we want to be seen

> reining in those negative emotions is a really important thing to do.

> Surprisingly, the study shows we respect people and think that they're saying more valuable things

## Collector's note

Trade-press coverage of Jen Overbeck's research on written language and perceived status. Analytical language and longer emails were associated with more perceived prestige; negative emotion and anger lowered perceived power and respect. The article doesn't give sample size or method beyond "linguistic cues", and a related search summary says the researcher warns this doesn't mean being wordy. For cardigan: this cuts against pure brevity. Cutting an email to the bone may cost the sender perceived standing, so trims should remove padding while keeping the reasoning (the analytical content) and avoid any trace of irritation when pushing back.
