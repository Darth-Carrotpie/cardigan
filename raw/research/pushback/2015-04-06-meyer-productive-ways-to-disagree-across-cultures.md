## Excerpts

> In China, protecting another person's face is more important than stating what you believe is correct.

> When I say "I disagree," I am debating Erin's position, not disapproving of her.

> In Japan for example, disagreeing with someone with white hair or rank is taboo.

> both your seniority and your age may impact the willingness of others to disagree openly

> In confrontation-avoiding cultures, people are more likely to use words like "sort of" or "partially".

> [...] avoiding stronger-sounding opinions of ideas such as "absolutely" or "totally"

> I would not recommend telling your French client "you are totally wrong".

> Putting ideas on a board anonymously then debating them with the group can facilitate disagreement

> conduct meetings before the main meeting, something especially important in East Asian cultures

> Sometimes, just a few words of explanation framing your behaviour can make all the difference

## Collector's note

Erin Meyer (INSEAD, author of The Culture Map) on how disagreement norms differ across cultures. Some cultures read open disagreement as debating ideas; others read it as a loss of face, especially when it's aimed at someone senior. She recommends softer qualifiers, private or pre-meeting channels, and briefly explaining your own style. For cardigan: the "question in one line" move is not culture-neutral. With hierarchical or confrontation-avoiding recipients the question should be softened, possibly kept to a private channel, or skipped in favour of just delivering the lighter default with an offer.
