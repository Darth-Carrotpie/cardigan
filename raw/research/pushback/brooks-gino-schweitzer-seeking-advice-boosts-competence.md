# Smart People Ask for (My) Advice: Seeking Advice Boosts Perceptions of Competence

> Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v61y2015i6p1421-1435.html
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: Unknown
> Author: Alison Wood Brooks, Francesca Gino, Maurice E. Schweitzer (Management Science 61(6), 2015)
> Type: study

## Excerpts

> Although individuals can derive substantial benefits from exchanging information and ideas, many individuals are reluctant to seek advice from others.

> We find that people are reticent to seek advice for fear of appearing incompetent. This fear, however, is misplaced.

> We demonstrate that individuals perceive those who seek advice as more competent than those who do not.

> This effect is moderated by task difficulty, advisor egocentrism, and advisor expertise.

> Individuals perceive those who seek advice as more competent when the task is difficult rather than when it is easy, when people seek advice from them personally rather than when they seek advice from others and when people seek advice from experts rather than from nonexperts or not at all.

## Collector's note

A set of experiments (Management Science, 2015) finding that people overestimate the competence penalty for asking. Advice-seekers are seen as more competent, but only when the task is hard and the question goes to the observer personally; on easy tasks there was no gain (secondary summaries say no loss either). For cardigan: a clarifying question to a manager or client is more likely to read as competence than as difficulty when it is substantive (about the purpose or audience of the deliverable) and addressed to them directly. Questions about trivial details get no such benefit.
