# Don't Bother Doing More Than You Promised

> Source: https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/dont-bother-doing-more-than-you-promised
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2014-11-17
> Author: David Jon Phillips / Chicago Booth Review (reporting on Epley & Gneezy, Social Psychological and Personality Science, 2014)
> Type: journalism

## Excerpts

> Breaking a promise is costly, but exceeding it might not be worth the effort.

> each study participant was paired with a volunteer who promised to solve 10 of the puzzles.

> As expected, participants valued keeping a promise more highly than breaking one.

> However, students said exceeded promises made them no happier.

> in their view, people who exceeded their promises worked no harder than people who simply kept promises.

> [...] may not produce the desired consequence beyond those obtained by simply keeping promises

> The research suggests that your boss will be just as happy if you simply fulfill your promise

## Collector's note

Reports Epley and Gneezy's "Worth Keeping but Not Exceeding" experiments: in the puzzle study (60 undergraduates per the article; volunteers secretly solved 5, 10 or 15 of a promised 10), broken promises were penalised but exceeded promises earned no extra credit, and the extra effort was not even perceived. Lab settings with small stakes; critics (e.g. Big Think) have called the scenarios artificial. For cardigan this is evidence that delivering exactly what was agreed captures most of the social value, so the bigger-than-asked version mostly costs effort without buying goodwill; the matching risk is under-delivering on the actual ask.
