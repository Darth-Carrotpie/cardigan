# Optimizing Your Team's Editing and Review Process

> Source: https://www.hurleywrite.com/optimizing-your-teams-editing-and-review-process/
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2024-02-27
> Author: Hurley Write, Inc. (business and technical writing training firm)
> Type: practitioner

## Excerpts

> Revisions, editing, and review are critical to producing successful written documents, but the process can easily turn wasteful, unhelpful, and unproductive. An effective review process requires a careful balancing act that recognizes the importance of editing (separate from writing) without inflating the process into a bloated mess.

> Think of it this way: you can reach "good enough" with just 20% of the effort it takes to reach "perfect." Strivers trying to get from "good enough" to "perfect" will then spend a disproportionate amount of time for marginal gains in quality.

> One way to streamline the editing process is to discern times when perfection is worth the investment of effort (perhaps a proposal that would elevate the business into a new playing field) versus when "good enough" is enough.

> In other words: don't over-edit; limit edits to the strictly necessary.

> Most people who must review and edit business documents do little more than slap the document on the table and pull out a red pen, but that's a recipe for wasting needless time on the process.

> Consider the goal for the document and its intended reader: the strategy should be to edit anywhere the document can be better aligned with its objectives and readers.

> For example, we once had a manager who spent an average of 16 hours a week (nearly half his workweek!) editing work produced by his engineers. This overloaded him and created a lot of stress and frustration among his team.

## Collector's note

A writing-training firm, which has a commercial interest, argues that review in organisations often bloats. It applies the Pareto principle (good enough takes about 20% of the effort of perfect), says to keep edits to what's strictly necessary, and says to judge edits by the document's goal and reader rather than by red-pen habit. The 16-hours-a-week case is a single anecdote from a client. This is evidence for the opposing side: cardigan should save heavy polish for high-stakes documents and judge every edit by whether it serves the reader and the purpose.
