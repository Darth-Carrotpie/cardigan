# Bottom Line Up Front: write to make decisions faster

> Source: https://matthewstrom.com/writing/bluf
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2020-05-17
> Author: Matt Ström-Awn (designer-leader), quoting US Army Regulation 25-50 and Air Force Handbook 33-337
> Type: practitioner

## Excerpts

> Army writing will be concise, organized, and to the point. Two essential requirements include putting the main point at the beginning of the correspondence (bottom line up front) and using the active voice (for example, "You are entitled to jump pay for the time you spent in training last year").

> Organizing: get your bottom line up front (most of the time). In nearly every communication situation, you need to state your bottom line early in the message. In a direct or deductive approach, state your position, main point or purpose up front, then go into the details that support your main point.

> To do our work, we all have to read a mass of papers. Nearly all of them are far too long. This wastes time, while energy has to be spent in looking for the essential points [...] But the saving of time will be great, while the discipline of setting out the real points concisely will prove an aid to clear thinking.

> The purpose of the bottom line is to capture the decisive moment of your argument, the sentence or two that most directly reflects your point of view. At the beginning of a document, the bottom line saves time by enabling the reader to respond immediately.

> Being direct and getting straight to the point isn't always the right approach. If you know your audience is already skeptical, putting the bottom line up front may cause the reader to ignore everything that comes after it.

> Being up front with your bottom line puts a lot of pressure on those first few sentences. Without context, any ambiguity is subject to misinterpretation.

> If you typically communicate in a more conversational style, your colleagues might interpret your brevity as sudden coldness. If you're already at a disadvantage due to office politics, gender bias, or cultural stereotypes, BLUF might not be the right approach.

## Collector's note

A practitioner essay that quotes the BLUF requirement in Army Regulation 25-50, the Air Force "Tongue and Quill" ("most of the time"), and Churchill's 1940 "BREVITY" memo (via Erik Larson). It argues that business email and memos should open with the decision-relevant point, then lists exceptions: skeptical readers, ambiguity when context is missing, and brevity read as coldness. For cardigan, this supports putting the ask or decision first, with a check for the audience and relationship before applying it. The military regulation was quoted here and not fetched directly.
