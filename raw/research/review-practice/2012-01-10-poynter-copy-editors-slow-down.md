# Copy editors' advice for the new year: 'Slow down'

> Source: https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2012/copy-editors-advice-for-the-new-year-slow-down/
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2012-01-10
> Author: Craig Silverman (Poynter / Regret the Error), quoting Pam Nelson (ACES) and Terence Walsh (The Frederick News-Post)
> Type: practitioner

## Excerpts

> Writers should use a personal accuracy checklist to review their work before passing it to an editor. The reality is newsrooms have fewer sets of eyes checking each piece of copy, so it's more essential than ever to be a great self editor.

> Among the items Nelson says editors (and writers!) need to check are: spelling of names and places, dates, numbers and math.

> 7. If a story uses a quote that seems off (a teacher misusing grammar, a politician or a law enforcement officer seeming to say the opposite of what you'd expect), check the quote with the writer. Sometimes a writer drops the "not" in a quote.

> Slow down — just a little. [...] When practical, however, the copy editor should take a few minutes to read the story and digest what is being said before making any changes beyond fixing an obvious typo. Making note of any questions you may have is a good idea, but read the whole story and make sure your question isn't addressed before posing it to the writer or assigning editor. Any "big-picture" issues regarding libel, fairness and the like should be raised as early as possible so that more reporting can be done, a senior editor can be consulted, or the story can be held until the concerns can be addressed.

> If it isn't broken, don't fix it. John McIntyre, night content production manager at The (Baltimore) Sun, says that a good copy editor knows when to leave things alone. [...] the copy editor should have a good reason why he or she made the change. Furthermore, inserting an error into a story is the cardinal sin of editing, one no editor wants to commit. In other words, make sure what you are "correcting" is actually incorrect.

> Editors save writers from making mistakes. It happens all the time. But it's also true that the editing process can introduce errors.

## Collector's note

Newsroom copy editors' advice collected by Craig Silverman: read the whole piece before changing anything, check whether a question is answered later before asking it, raise big-picture risks (libel, fairness) early, and leave correct copy alone because editing can introduce errors. This maps closely onto ponytail's "read fully, then be lazy": cardigan should read the whole document or thread before suggesting edits, verify names, dates, numbers and quotes, and avoid changes that have no clear reason.
