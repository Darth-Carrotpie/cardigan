# The increased danger of 'reply all': a warning for in-house counsel

> Source: https://corrs.com.au/insights/the-increased-danger-of-reply-all-a-warning-for-in-house-counsel
> Collected: 2026-10-31
> Published: 2021-04-21
> Author: Corrs Chambers Westgarth (Australian law firm)
> Type: practitioner

## Excerpts

> Privilege is an important legal right but it is also very easy to waive.

> Email chains that evolve from having a legal purpose to having a non-legal (or mixed) purpose may now result in a waiver of privilege of legal advice contained in the chain.

> In-house counsel (and those who seek their advice) need to carefully consider who to address their emails to and should consider starting a new email chain rather than forwarding lengthy chains to multiple colleagues.

> This final point is the most important consideration to arise from the case. It means that if an email is sent to 10 recipients, one of whom is a lawyer, only the copy of the email that goes to the lawyer will be privileged (assuming it seeks legal advice). The remaining copies of the email will not be privileged even though they contain exactly the same content as the copy sent to the lawyer.

> Identify who actually needs access to your advice and consider emailing them individually. This approach may also help prevent lengthy email chains circulating around your organisation.

> In circumstances where your colleagues are requesting legal advice from you, consider asking them to restrict the purpose of their emails with you to that sole purpose.

> It is a common misconception that copying a lawyer on an email is sufficient for a communication to attract privilege.

## Collector's note

A law-firm briefing on the Australian case TEC Hedland v Pilbara Infrastructure ([2020] WASC 364), which follows an English Court of Appeal approach. It treats an email to several recipients as a separate communication with each one, so privilege attaches only to the copy sent to the lawyer, and only if its dominant purpose is legal advice. The firm recommends writing to the people who actually need the advice, starting a new chain instead of forwarding long ones, and keeping each email to one purpose. The ruling is specific to Australia, but it gives cardigan a legal reason to check recipients and scope before sending: forwarding a whole thread, or reply-all, can widen exposure without anyone noticing.
